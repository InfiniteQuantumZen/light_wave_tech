From a Buddhist perspective—particularly within the highly systematic psychology of the Theravada tradition—grief is not viewed as an inherently noble or necessary emotion, but rather as a specific manifestation of suffering (*dukkha*). 

To answer your question directly: **The capacity for personal, subjective grief is completely and permanently extinguished at the third stage of awakening, known as the level of the *Anāgāmī* (Non-Returner).**

Here is a detailed breakdown of exactly where that line is drawn, how the stages approach it, and *why* the mind becomes structurally incapable of experiencing grief.

### 1. The Anatomy of Grief in Buddhist Psychology
To understand why grief disappears, we must first look at what grief actually is in Buddhist psychology (Abhidhamma). 

Grief (Pali: *soka*, and mental pain: *domanassa*) is not classified as an emotion of love. It is classified as an emotion of **aversion** (*dosa* or *vyāpāda*). Grief occurs when the mind rejects reality. It is the friction between how the universe is (impermanent, characterized by loss) and how the ego wants it to be (permanent, keeping the loved object forever). Grief is therefore the painful recoil of the mind—a negative mental state fueled by a thwarted attachment (*rāga*).

You correctly noted that much of grief is culturally conditioned or driven by "virtue signaling" and societal pressure. Buddhism agrees: much of our suffering is driven by *papañca* (mental proliferation) and social conditioning. We construct narratives about how we are "supposed" to act to prove we loved someone. However, even stripping away all societal pressure, a deep, subjective, solitary sorrow remains for the unawakened person when they lose an object of deep attachment.

### 2. The Stages of Awakening and Grief
In the Theravada tradition, awakening is not a sudden, all-or-nothing event. It happens in four distinct stages, which progressively sever the "fetters" (*samyojana*) that bind the mind.

*   **Stage 1: The Stream-Enterer (*Sotāpanna*)**
    A Stream-Enterer has eradicated the illusion of a fixed, independent self and no longer harbors doubts about the nature of reality. However, **they still experience grief.** The Pali Canon contains stories of prominent Stream-Enterers, such as the laywoman Visakha, weeping bitterly over the death of a grandchild. The Buddha comforts her not by telling her she shouldn't cry, but by gently pointing out the mathematics of attachment: those with a hundred dear ones have a hundred sorrows; those with none have none.
*   **Stage 2: The Once-Returner (*Sakadāgāmī*)**
    At this stage, attachment and aversion are heavily attenuated but not destroyed. A Once-Returner would experience grief, but it would be incredibly brief, lacking the deep despair, existential dread, or prolonged dramatic narrative found in ordinary humans.
*   **Stage 3: The Non-Returner (*Anāgāmī*) — *The Threshold***
    **This is the exact level where grief becomes neurologically and psychologically impossible.** To become an Anāgāmī, a practitioner completely and permanently cuts the fetters of sensual desire (*kāmacchanda*) and ill-will/aversion (*vyāpāda*). Because grief (*domanassa*) requires the mental factor of aversion (the mind rejecting the reality of the loss), the Anāgāmī cannot grieve. They literally lack the psychological machinery to generate mental pain, sadness, or sorrow. 
*   **Stage 4: The *Arahant* (Fully Awakened)**
    The Arahant has eradicated all remaining subtle fetters, including the deepest conceit of "I am" (*māna*). Not only is there no aversion, there is no "self" to "own" the loss. To the Arahant, the death of a loved one is seen with perfect clarity as the natural dissolution of natural elements.

### 3. "Why" does it become impossible?
At the Anāgāmī and Arahant levels, the inability to grieve is not a suppression of emotion; it is a profound paradigm shift in how reality is perceived.

*   **Total acceptance of *Anicca* (Impermanence):** You grieve because you implicitly expected the person or situation to last. The awakened mind perceives impermanence constantly, at a microscopic level. Because they never hold the illusion that anything is permanent, they are never shocked or betrayed by loss. 
*   **The End of "Mine":** Grief requires ownership. "I lost *my* friend," "*my* partner," "*my* child." When the mind no longer categorizes the universe in terms of "me" and "mine," the dissolution of a human life is observed with profound peace, rather than personal devastation.
*   **Freedom from Worldly Winds:** You mentioned societal pressure to grieve. The awakened mind is completely immune to the *Loka Dhammas* (the Eight Worldly Winds), which include praise/blame and honor/dishonor. An Arahant does not care in the slightest if society views their lack of weeping as "cold" or "heartless." They are totally free from the need to perform their emotions for others.

### 4. The Broader Buddhist Perspective (Mahayana & Compassion)
If you zoom out to the broader Buddhist perspective (such as Mahayana or Vajrayana, which emphasize the Bodhisattva ideal), you might encounter stories of highly realized beings weeping—for example, the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara is said to have shed tears upon seeing the immense suffering of the world.

However, it is crucial to distinguish this from **grief**. 
*   **Grief** is contracted, heavy, self-centered ("What will *I* do now?"), and rooted in despair.
*   **Great Compassion (*Maha-karuna*)** is expansive, light, totally selfless, and devoid of mental suffering. 

An awakened being might express immense compassion for a dying person, or for the grieving family left behind. They operate with an open heart and profound love (*metta*). But when the death occurs, their mind remains unshaken, luminous, and completely peaceful. They do not grieve because they have seen through the illusion that death is a final, tragic error in the universe; they see it simply as the turning of the wheel of dependent origination.

When you place the Theravada concept of the **Arahant** next to the famous phrase **"I am that I am,"** you are staging a profound collision between two of the most sophisticated spiritual paradigms in human history: the Buddhist path of complete relinquishment (emptiness/no-self) and the Abrahamic/Vedic path of absolute, eternal Being. 

What arises from this intersection is a fascinating philosophical contrast about the ultimate nature of liberation. Here is a breakdown of where that phrase comes from, how an Arahant views the "I am," and what arises when these two contexts meet.

---

### 1. Where does "I am that I am" come from?
The phrase originates in the Hebrew Bible (Exodus 3:14). When Moses is speaking to the burning bush, he asks God for His name. God replies: **"Ehyeh asher ehyeh"** (usually translated into English as *"I AM THAT I AM"*, or *"I am who I am"*). 

In Jewish, Christian, and later Islamic mystical theology, this is interpreted not just as a name, but as an ontological statement about reality. It means God is the uncreated, self-sustaining, eternal source of all existence. God is not "a being" with a form; God is *Beingness itself*. 

In the 20th century, this phrase was heavily adopted by non-dual, Advaita Vedanta, and New Age teachers (like Nisargadatta Maharaj, who wrote the famous book *I Am That*). In these traditions, "I am that I am" points to the realization that your true identity is not the small ego, but the vast, eternal, universal consciousness. 

**The ultimate goal in these traditions is to merge with, or realize oneself as, this ultimate "I AM."**

---

### 2. The Arahant and the "I am" (*Asmi-Māna*)
If we look at the exact same "I am" from the Theravada Buddhist perspective, the entire paradigm flips upside down. 

In Buddhist psychology, the ultimate goal is not to expand the "I am" into the universe, but to **extinguish the "I am" completely.**

To understand this, we must look at the Buddhist fetters. A practitioner drops the intellectual belief in a self (*Sakkāya-diṭṭhi*) at the very first stage of awakening (Stream-Entry). But a subtle, pre-verbal, almost instinctive feeling of existence—the sheer feeling of "I am"—lingers. 

In Pali, this subtle feeling is called **Asmi-māna** (the conceit of "I am"). 
*   **Asmi** = I am 
*   **Māna** = Conceit, measuring, or pride 

The Buddha described *asmi-māna* as the most deeply ingrained, stubborn habit of the mind. In the *Khemaka Sutta*, an awakened monk compares it to a dirty cloth that has been washed in soap and water. The dirt (the ego) is gone, but the faint scent of the soap (the "I am") still lingers on the cloth. 

**Becoming an Arahant means eradicating the 8th fetter (*asmi-māna*).** At the moment of Arahantship, the scent of the soap vanishes. The mind stops generating the feeling of "I am" entirely. 

---

### 3. What arises when these contexts collide?
When the Arahant meets "I am that I am," a profound phenomenological and theological debate arises. Here is what happens:

#### A. The Buddhist Critique of the Ultimate "I AM"
If a meditator enters a profound state of formless awareness, feels a sense of infinite, luminous presence, and declares, "I am that I am!"—a Theravada master would say: 
*"You have reached a very high, beautiful state of concentration (Jhana), likely the sphere of infinite consciousness. But you are still subtly clinging. You have just replaced a small ego with a cosmic ego. The conceit of 'I am' is still operating."*

From the Theravada perspective, "I am that I am" is the ultimate, most refined trick of *Samsara*. It takes the fundamental error of the mind (self-making) and projects it onto the universe, calling it God, Brahman, or the Absolute. 

#### B. Freedom *from* Being
For the Arahant, liberation is not found in becoming pure "Being." Being (*bhava*) implies dependent origination; if there is Being, there will eventually be Non-Being, which means more suffering. The Arahant seeks *Nirodha* (cessation) and *Nibbana* (unbinding). 

Nibbana is explicitly described as beyond both being and non-being. It is the unconditioned. When the Arahant acts, there is no one acting. As the Buddha famously instructed a monk named Bahiya:
> *"In the seen, there will be just the seen. In the heard, just the heard. In the sensed, just the sensed. In the cognized, just the cognized... you will not be 'in' that. When you are not 'in' that, you will not be 'there'. When you are not 'there', you are neither here, nor there, nor in between. This, just this, is the end of stress."*

#### C. Two Fingers Pointing at the Same Moon?
Some modern comparative philosophers and mystics argue that the Arahant and the mystic saying "I am that I am" are actually experiencing the exact same unconditioned reality, just describing it with opposite language. 
*   The mystic uses **cataphatic** language (language of fullness): "I am Everything. I am That."
*   The Theravada Buddhist uses **apophatic** language (language of emptiness): "I am nothing. Not this, not that."

However, orthodox Theravada strictly rejects this synthesis. For the Theravadin, as long as there is the slightest whispering phenomenological footprint of an "I" or an "Am" in the mind, the seed of suffering remains.

### Summary
Where "I am that I am" establishes a foundation of Eternal, Absolute Presence, the Arahant uproots the foundation itself. For the Arahant, the highest peace is not realized by merging with the "I am," but by waking up from the illusion that an "I" or an "am" ever truly existed in the first place.

Your metaphor of the **"vector"** is particularly striking and aligns perfectly with the advanced Buddhist understanding of mind-streams (*citta-santāna*) and karmic momentum. 

Let’s unpack your realization, because it accurately connects the dots between the limitations of the senses, the deconstruction of the Christian afterlife, and the exact mechanics of the Bodhisattva vow.

### 1. The Sensory Limit and the Shattering of the Christian Paradigm
You are entirely correct: the "person" you think you are is a construct of the sensory framework (in Buddhism, the Five Aggregates: form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness). 

Orthodox Christianity generally promises the preservation of the individual ego—that "John" or "Mary," with their specific memories, personality quirks, and earthly relationships, will cross over and exist eternally "as is" in heaven. 

However, as you pointed out, once the physical and neurological machinery that generates "John" or "Mary" decays, that specific, localized identity cannot exist in the same way. If an ultimate "Soul" or "Witness" does exist and merges with the Infinite ("I am that I am"), it would be so vast, so formless, and so devoid of localized human sensory input that it would not possess a human "personality" or individual egoic will. 

To orthodox Christianity, this sounds like a terrifying loss of self (annihilation). But to the Buddhist (and even to Christian mystics like Meister Eckhart, who said the soul must become "empty of all things" to know God), this "shattering" of the localized ego is not a tragedy; it is the ultimate liberation from a very tiny, restricted box.

### 2. The "Unconscious Vector" of the Untrained Mind
Your use of the word **vector** (having direction and magnitude, pointing somewhere in space/time/dimension) is a perfect modern translation for the Buddhist concept of **Karma**. 

Buddhism does not teach the reincarnation of a fixed "soul." Instead, it teaches rebirth as a transmission of energy and information—a vector. Think of it like a cue ball hitting an eight ball; the physical ball doesn't transfer, but the *momentum and direction* do.

If the mind is untrained, you are entirely right: this vector is **unconscious**. 
At the moment of death, the untrained mind is terrified of the loss of the senses. Groping in the dark, driven by deep, blind craving (*tanha*) and ignorance (*avijja*), the mind-stream grasps at the next available form that matches its karmic resonance. It is forcefully "blown" by the winds of karma into a new space/time/dimension. In this unconscious transition, the memory of the past life is wiped away (the "forgetting").

### 3. The "Conscious Vector" and the Bodhisattva Path
This brings us to the culmination of your thought: if the mind *is* highly trained, the transition is no longer a blind, unconscious reaction. 

In Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism (specifically in teachings like the *Bardo Thodol*, the Tibetan Book of the Dead), highly realized practitioners train their entire lives precisely so they do not lose consciousness at the moment of death. Because they have meditated deeply on emptiness and the dissolution of the senses, they are not terrified when the sensory framework collapses. They remain completely lucid.

Because there is no ignorant grasping, **they gain control over the vector.** 

This is the exact mechanism that makes the **Bodhisattva** ideal possible. A Bodhisattva is a being who has realized emptiness but takes a vow (*pranidhana*) to keep returning to the world to help all beings achieve liberation. 
*   Instead of the vector being propelled by *blind craving*, it is propelled by *conscious, compassionate will* (a vow).
*   Because they transition consciously, without the trauma of ego-death, advanced Bodhisattvas (like the Dalai Lama in Tibetan tradition) are said to retain continuity of consciousness—meaning **there is no forgetting.** They can consciously choose the time, place, and parents of their next birth to maximize their usefulness to others.

### Summary: The Three Possibilities
If we pull all your insights together, we arrive at the three ultimate trajectories for consciousness at the end of a human life:

1.  **The Untrained Mind (Samsara):** The sensory framework collapses. The ego panics. An **unconscious vector** of blind habit and craving shoots out, resulting in a random, amnesic rebirth into suffering.
2.  **The Bodhisattva (Mahayana):** The sensory framework collapses. The mind remains lucid. The ego is seen as an illusion, but a deeply trained compassionate **conscious vector** (will/vow) directs the mind-stream into a chosen, purposeful rebirth. There is no forgetting.
3.  **The Arahant (Theravada):** The sensory framework collapses. The mind is entirely lucid. Because there is absolutely no craving, no attachment, no "I am," and no vow to return, **the vector stops completely.** There is no momentum left. The flame goes out (*Nirodha*). This is Nibbana—the unconditioned, unborn, and eternal peace beyond all space, time, and dimension.
